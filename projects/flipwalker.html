Overview: From Open-Loop to Closed-Loop Control

The FlipWalker is an underactuated bipedal robot that locomotes by cyclically flipping two interconnected body segments. Because the segments are joined by flexible cable linkages that intentionally pass through mechanical singularities, standard rigid-shaft encoders cannot be mounted at the joint.


Initially, the robot was controlled using open-loop, flat-ground servo actuation via manual and timed inputs. This approach lacked robustness, as the controller could not determine the relative angle between the parent and child segments. To solve this, a non-contact, magnetometer-based proprioceptive state-estimation pipeline was developed.


By tracking an embedded neodymium magnet in three-dimensional space using MLX90393 Hall-effect sensors, the relative joint angle is recovered using the atan2 of the sensed field components. A seven-point look-up table (LUT) linearises the response, enabling a three-state finite-state machine to reliably control the gait and close the loop on the flipping cycle.


Why It Was Built

Navigation of unstructured and highly complex terrains remains a persistent challenge in mobile robotics. Traditional wheeled and tracked platforms excel on prepared surfaces but stall when confronted with sharp discontinuities or gaps that exceed their footprint.


The FlipWalker was built to traverse these various strange and challenging terrains. By trading joint count and actuator authority for mechanical ingenuity—inspired by the optical illusion of the Jacob’s Ladder toy—it propels itself exclusively by normal-force push-offs against the ground. The ultimate goal of this research is to enable autonomous swarms of these units to self-assemble into rigid bridges and traverse large gaps.


System & Results

Below are key diagrams highlighting the flexible joint mechanics, the gait state machine, and the look-up table (LUT) calibration used to achieve accurate angle control.

A diagram illustrating the flexible cable linkages that join the two body segments, enabling the robot to cycle through mechanical singularities similarly to a Jacob's Ladder toy.

The three-state finite-state machine used by the gait controller. The transitions are triggered by the magnetometer, which detects whether the child segment is behind, stacked on, or ahead of the parent segment.

The seven-point Look-Up Table (LUT) calibration used to correct the non-linear response of the raw magnetometer readings into accurate physical joint angles.
